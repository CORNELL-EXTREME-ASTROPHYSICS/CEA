About the group

Cornell Extreme Astrophysics is a research group in Cornell’s Department of Astronomy studying pulsars, gravitational waves, and fast radio bursts — using some of the largest radio telescopes on Earth as precision instruments.

The group is led by Prof. James M. Cordes, the George Feldstein Professor of Astronomy, and Prof. Shami Chatterjee, Associate Professor of Astronomy, together with a rotating roster of graduate students, postdoctoral researchers, and undergraduates working across theory, instrumentation, and observation.

Our central tool is pulsar timing: millisecond pulsars behave as extraordinarily stable cosmic clocks, and by monitoring an array of them for years at a time, tiny, correlated deviations in their arrival times can reveal a gravitational-wave background washing over the galaxy — most likely the combined hum of thousands of merging supermassive black hole binaries. This is the same technique behind NANOGrav, the Physics Frontiers Center the group helps lead, and the wider International Pulsar Timing Array (IPTA) collaboration that pools data from timing arrays on every continent.

The group’s reach extends beyond gravitational waves. A large pulsar survey run from the Arecibo Observatory turned up the first known repeating fast radio burst, FRB 121102 — a discovery that was followed by its localization to a dwarf galaxy roughly three billion light-years away, work that put the group on the cover of Nature twice. Members of the group have also traced ultra-high-velocity pulsars flung out of the galaxy at over 1,000 km/s, and now contribute to the Deep Synoptic Array, a next-generation instrument built to catch and localize FRBs as they happen.

A smaller thread of the group’s work, pursued through Cornell’s Carl Sagan Institute, looks outward in a different sense — toward the habitability of planets around other stars. It is not the group’s focus, but it shares the same instruments and the same appetite for signals buried in noise.

What PTAs listen for

A supermassive black hole binary, slowly spiraling inward, is the kind of source thought to generate the stochastic gravitational-wave background pulsar timing arrays are built to detect.

A short history

Milestones in the field

A few of the results the group’s work sits alongside — from the original theoretical prediction to the most recent detection.

1983

Hellings & Downs predict the signature

The angular correlation pattern a gravitational-wave background should imprint across pairs of pulsars is derived — the template every pulsar timing array still searches for.

2007

NANOGrav founded

North American Nanohertz Observatory for Gravitational Waves forms as a collaboration, later becoming an NSF Physics Frontiers Center.

2016

The first repeating fast radio burst

A large pulsar survey using the Arecibo telescope turns up FRB 121102 — the first FRB ever seen to repeat.

2017

FRB 121102 localized

Precise localization pins the repeating burst to a dwarf galaxy roughly three billion light-years away — the first FRB traced to a host galaxy.

2020

Arecibo Observatory collapses

After 57 years of service and a role in decades of pulsar and radar astronomy, the Arecibo telescope’s instrument platform falls, ending observations.

2023

Evidence for the gravitational-wave background

NANOGrav, together with PTAs worldwide, reports evidence of a signal matching the Hellings–Downs pattern in 15 years of pulsar timing data.
