01 · Nanohertz gravitational waves

Pulsar timing arrays

Millisecond pulsars rotate hundreds of times per second with clockwork stability. By timing the arrival of their radio pulses over years, and comparing many pulsars scattered across the sky at once, a galaxy-sized gravitational-wave detector emerges — sensitive to wavelengths measured in light-years, far below anything a ground-based interferometer like LIGO can reach.

The signature to look for is angular: pulsar pairs close together on the sky should show correlated timing residuals, pairs far apart should show a different, predictable correlation — the Hellings–Downs curve shown on the home page. The group contributes timing data and analysis to NANOGrav and the wider International Pulsar Timing Array.

02 · Millisecond flashes

Fast radio bursts

Fast radio bursts are millisecond-duration radio flashes, most of them from galaxies far beyond our own, bright enough to briefly outshine everything else in the radio sky. The delay between their arrival at different frequencies — caused by ionized gas along the line of sight — encodes how much material the signal crossed to reach us.

A large pulsar survey run from Arecibo turned up FRB 121102, the first burst ever seen to repeat, which the group went on to localize to a dwarf galaxy roughly three billion light-years away — the first time any FRB was pinned to a host. That work continues today through the Deep Synoptic Array, built to catch and localize bursts in real time.

03 · From dish to detection

Radio astronomy & instrumentation

None of the above works without an observing chain built to pull faint, structured signals out of a much louder radio sky. The group has built and used pieces of that chain at facilities across the country — and, for 57 years, at Arecibo.
